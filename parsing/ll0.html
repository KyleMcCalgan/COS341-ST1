Clarification

There's no "LL(0)" step in this textbook — here's why

I searched the full text of Introduction to Compiler Design for it: no "Algorithm", section, or figure is named LL(0) anywhere in the chapter on parsing. That's not an omission — it's because LL(0) parsing is a real but almost useless degenerate case, and naming it mostly serves to sharpen what the "1" in LL(1) is actually buying you.

What LL(0) would mean, literally

Follow the naming convention: Left-to-right input, Leftmost derivation, 0 symbols of lookahead. Zero lookahead means the parser must choose which production to apply for a nonterminal N without looking at the input at all — not even the next symbol.

Consequence For LL(0) to work, every nonterminal must have at most one production — otherwise there is, by definition, no information available to choose between them.

A grammar where every nonterminal has exactly one production doesn't really have "choices" left in it at all — it deterministically unfolds into one fixed shape (modulo recursion depth). That's a vanishingly small class of grammars — essentially none of the grammars that describe real programming-language constructs (an if with or without an else, an expression that may or may not have a following operator, a statement list that may or may not continue) can be written this way, because those are exactly the situations where you need at least one symbol of input to know which shape you're in.

Why the field jumps straight to LL(1)

The moment a nonterminal has two productions — even the most basic case, "is this list empty or not" — you need to distinguish them somehow. The cheapest possible piece of information that could ever distinguish them is the very next input symbol. That's exactly one symbol of lookahead. LL(1) is, in effect, the smallest lookahead that makes the method non-trivial, which is why it's the standard entry point rather than LL(0).

Higher values of k (LL(2), LL(k) in general) exist and extend the same idea by committing to a production based on the next k symbols instead of 1 — but they aren't covered in this textbook's chapter either, and grow the table size and complexity fast for limited practical gain, which is part of why SLR (covered in full on the SLR page) is usually reached for instead once LL(1) isn't enough.

If the test scope really does mean LL(0) Double check whether "LL0" in your notes actually refers to something else that sounds similar — most commonly this is a mix-up with LR(0), which is a real and heavily-examined concept: it's the item/automaton construction that SLR is built on top of (Steps A–C on the SLR page), before FOLLOW sets are used to add reduce actions. If your lecture used the term "LL0" to mean the plain LR(0) automaton construction, the SLR page's CLOSURE/GOTO section is the one to study.
